\begin{abstract}
Parametric polymorphism, type classes and structural recursion are the tools with which a functional programmer says what a program does and has a type checker hold it to that.
\Cpp{23}, which runs much of machine learning and most embedded devices, has the material for them: compile-time evaluation of ordinary functions and of most of the standard library, concepts, and folds over ranges.
It does not, however, hold a generic function to its declared constraints: the body may use any operation its type argument happens to have.

This paper characterises a fragment of \Cpp{23} as it ships, with no language modification, compiler extension or tool to install: an embedded language whose domain is System~F and whose programs are ordinary \Cpp{23} programs.
The fragment is type-class-style code under three rules, which we call the Juliet Posture (Jlt):
i) values are copyable data and functions are pure and evaluable at compile time;
ii) generic code varies with a type only through that type's concepts and traits;
iii) recursion is structural, on a type or on a natural number that counts down.
Its kernel, Featherweight Jlt (FJlt), translates into System~F by dictionary passing, as Haskell's type classes do, and the translation is total and preserves types and evaluation, so an FJlt program inherits termination, parametricity and a value.
What Jlt writes beyond the kernel is sugar, named with its desugaring, and that the desugaring keeps meaning is the paper's working hypothesis.
Declared laws license overloads, and overload resolution composes them: in one program the action law and associativity, neither aware of the other, turn a fold of $n$ steps into one of $\log n$, even at compile time, where no optimiser runs.
Along the way the paper shows that \Cpp{20} concepts with overlapping overloads resolve incoherently in GHC's sense, and adopts GHC's rule.
The evidence is proof sketches, listings that compile and run at both binding times under a test in the public repository, their compiled output, and a library whose core a linter checks against the rules.

The claim is one of existence: \Cpp{23} already contains an embedding of a fragment of System~F, held by the compiler where the standard checks and by the programmer's discipline elsewhere, and available today wherever a stock \Cpp{23} compiler is.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The case for functional programming has been made the same way for decades, and it is a good case; even a compiler's own intermediate form, static single assignment, is a functional language~\cite{appel1998ssa}.
A function that depends on its arguments alone can be replaced by its value: programs can be read as equations, rewritten by them, and reasoned about by them~\cite{hughes1989why}.
A function that is polymorphic in a type cannot inspect that type, so its type alone says a great deal about what it does~\cite{wadler1989theorems}.
A class of types with operations and laws lets generic code be written once against the operations and reasoned about by the laws~\cite{wadler1989adhoc}.
The two are one mechanism with two sources of equations: a free theorem is proved from the type alone, for every term, and a law is declared of an instance.
Either becomes a rewrite by Leibniz's law, which Rule~1 conserves (Section~\ref{sec:posture}).
For the declared laws Birkhoff's theorem makes the reasoning complete: an equation follows from the laws by equational reasoning exactly when it holds in every model of them~\cite{birkhoff1935structure,burris1981universalalgebra}.
A class with laws is an equational theory and its instances the models, a variety, and a stronger class has fewer instances, so the constraints describe the structure and the structure licenses the optimisation.
The goal is correctness by construction in Dijkstra's sense~\cite{dijkstra1976discipline}.

The case is usually made in a language built around it.
This paper makes it in a mainstream systems language instead, \Cpp{23}.
Much of machine learning and most embedded devices run on \Cpp{}, and a growing number of the people who write it arrive from Python with a functional vocabulary.
For them the repository binds the library's combinators into Python as handles to the compiled core, so the laws declared in \Cpp{} hold in an interactive session: the specialisations they license run there at run time, and only what the compiler evaluates of them is fixed when the bindings are built (Section~\ref{sec:binding}).
The most widely written functional language may well be SQL's \texttt{SELECT}, a declarative query over relations~\cite{iso9075sql}, whose algebra is Codd's~\cite{codd1970relational}, after Tarski's calculus of relations~\cite{tarski1941calculus}.
A functional fragment of \Cpp{23} is worth drawing because the whole is too large to work in with confidence, and worth drawing now because \Cpp{23} is the first standard with every ingredient: compile-time evaluation of ordinary functions and of most of the standard library, concepts, and folds over ranges.
Performance is why one comes to such a language in the first place, by its zero-overhead principle~\cite{stroustrup2005abstraction},\footnote{Stroustrup has since called the phrase an understatement: ``It's compiled away. This is why I talk about zero overhead abstraction, and people are beginning to take me to task for that because that's understating the ability of the \Cpp{} compiler. We can do negative overhead abstraction''~\cite{stroustrup2026peterman}.
Listing~\ref{lst:desugar} is a case: the chain, the arrow and the matrix arithmetic compile away to the Fibonacci recurrence.} and the posture does not affect it: an optimising compiler already reasons about a program, by abstract interpretation~\cite{cousot1977abstract} and by algebraic laws it is told it may assume, as \texttt{-ffast-math} lets it for floating point.
What the posture addresses is the other cost, the language's size.
Alignment with the posture is easier to check than a decade ago: for example, a language model of code~\cite{chen2021evaluating} reads every change against its rules.
And while this is not a hard proof but only a read, it is often a thorough read (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\Cpp{} is not a theorem prover, but its compiler performs checks of the same kind: a compile-time check is a claim the compiler verifies, and the worth of such a checker when the author is not the one trusted had a recent highlight, machine-generated proofs~\cite{hubert2025olympiad} in Lean~\cite{demoura2021lean} accepted because the checker, not the author, said so.
The search that found those proofs is a black box, and a language model's account of its own reasoning need not be the reasoning it did: its chain of thought can leave out what drove the answer, and circuit tracing finds computations its explanation misdescribes~\cite{turpin2023unfaithful,lindsey2025biology}.
What makes the output usable is that it is no black box but an artefact a small checker verifies, an interpretable model in place of an explained one, and a registry of Lean-verified mathematics is arranged that way: a short statement a human reads, checked against a proof of any length, which a machine may find correct and still unreadable~\cite{rudin2019stop,tao2026palomar}.
A program is such an artefact too: a machine runs an executable with no interpretation, so every claim about it is a claim about something formal, regardless of whether that actually is what its author intended.
For generic code, \Cpp{} lacks just such a check.
\cppinline{concept}s as proposed for \Cpp{0x} checked a template's definition against its constraints~\cite{gregor2006concepts,dosreis2006specifying}, but as standardised in \Cpp{20} they check only its uses, when the template is instantiated~\cite{stroustrup2009remove}, so a template's body may use operations its constraints do not name.
The designers named the problem at the outset, in 2003: ``crucial aspects of the programmer's understanding of a template cannot be expressed directly in the code for compilers and other tools to use''~\cite{stroustrup2003concepts}.
\Cpp{23} therefore contains the material for a typed functional fragment but does not enforce its boundary, and this paper documents a discipline that does, recovering some of what a functional language guarantees at the cost of three rules.
The cost is paid once, in how a program is written, and what it buys is paid back at every use: a law holds in every model of it, a specialisation it licenses pays on every call, and a checked statement serves every reader.

The fragment is the \emph{Juliet Posture}, or Jlt, and it has two boundaries, both drawn here.
The outer boundary is drawn by the three rules: Jlt is a proper subset of \Cpp{23} defined by these three rules.
We call it a posture because it asks only for a style of programming: every ingredient is in the ISO standard~\cite{cpp23standard}, implemented by toolchains in wide use, and what Jlt adds is the programmer's discipline.
The compiler holds some of the rules and the programmer the rest, and a linter helps but is not required (Table~\ref{tab:posture}).
The inner boundary is drawn by the translation: the \emph{kernel}, Featherweight Jlt (FJlt), is the part of Jlt on which a translation to System~F ($\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket$) is defined, and it inherits from System~F termination, parametricity and a value.
Between the two lies \emph{sugar}, in Landin's word~\cite{landin1964mechanical}, which this paper names and characterises without proof (Appendix~\ref{app:fjlt:closure}), the arrangement being the Haskell Report's, a kernel and the translations into it~\cite{marlow2010haskell}.

Jlt is thus an embedded domain-specific language in Hudak's sense~\cite{hudak1996building}, whose domain is System~F and whose host is \Cpp{23}.
Its programs are ordinary \Cpp{23} programs, type-checked and compiled by an unmodified toolchain, Clang~23 in this case (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).
Being a strict subset of \Cpp{23}, it is not a language this paper designs and develops as an architect might develop a house.
Instead it characterises a fragment of a pre-existing ecosystem, as a traveller might discover a city.
The claim here is one of existence: \Cpp{23} contains an embedding of a sizeable fragment of System~F, and the listings exhibit it.
No claim is made that Jlt is unique or canonical, although it seems likely that another fragment drawn to the same ends would look much like it.
As in the tagless-final style of Carette, Kiselyov and Shan~\cite{carette2009finally}, the embedded terms are generic code over an interface, here a \cppinline{concept}, and their interpretations are its models.
The posture's name is a nod to structural typing~\cite{pierce2002tapl,cardelli1996typesystems}: in \Cpp{23} a \cppinline{concept} is a predicate on a type's structure, not its lineage, and so a rose by any other name would smell as sweet~\cite{shakespeare1597romeo}.
Figure~\ref{fig:arrows} sketches the arrows between these pieces.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
The contribution is a map from a known discipline onto existing mainstream functionality, not a piece of software.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The posture: three rules on standard \Cpp{23}, each with what the compiler checks of it and what it leaves to the author (Section~\ref{sec:posture}).
\item Featherweight Jlt, the kernel: a core calculus whose syntax is a subset of \Cpp{23} as written, with typing, evaluation and a translation into System~F. Three theorems about the translation and three corollaries, with proof sketches (Section~\ref{sec:guarantees}, Appendix~\ref{app:fjlt}).
\item An adequacy argument from \Cpp{} to the calculus, which states its gaps rather than writing around them (Sections~\ref{sec:translation:adequacy} and~\ref{sec:limits}), and the sugar, named with its desugaring (Appendix~\ref{app:fjlt:closure}).
\item An observation about \Cpp{20} concepts: with overlapping constrained overloads, a generic function's behaviour can depend on a constraint it never declared, which is GHC's incoherent instance resolution, by default and silently.
Jlt adopts GHC's rule, and the repository's tool reports a call at a value of parameter type that breaks it (Appendix~\ref{app:fjlt:typing}, Section~\ref{sec:limits}).
\item The exhibits: every listing in this paper compiles and runs under a stock compiler at both binding times, by a test in the accompanying repository~\cite{Krautler_dedekind_2026}, and is held to the three rules by a mandatory review against them and by a mechanical linter in the same repository: that is what compliance means here.
\end{enumerate}
Not contributed: the translation, which is Wadler and Blott's~\cite{wadler1989adhoc}; the calculus-with-concepts, which is Siek and Lumsdaine's $F^G$~\cite{siek2005essential}; and complete definition checking: Section~\ref{sec:limits} says what the repository's linter reads and what it misses.

\paragraph{How to read this paper.}
The paper is written for authors of generic \Cpp{} libraries first, with System~F as the warranty and a rule for their overloads; for those who audit \Cpp{} against a policy and need to know what a tool checks; for readers of programming languages in the line of concepts and type classes; and for researchers who arrive from Python and want the performance of \Cpp{} with a guard rail.
The sections follow the arrows of Figure~\ref{fig:arrows}, one each.
Section~\ref{sec:posture} is the right hook, the rules that carve Jlt out of \Cpp{23}; Section~\ref{sec:translation:adequacy} is the left hook, the sugar: which Jlt programs are kernel programs; Section~\ref{sec:translation} is $\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket$, the translation of Featherweight Jlt into System~F; Section~\ref{sec:guarantees} is \emph{eval}, the theorems read back from System~F; Section~\ref{sec:binding} is \texttt{clang++} and \emph{run}, one program at both binding times, and how instantiation relates to the translation.
The listings follow one example through the paper, and Figure~\ref{fig:structures} shows where its structures live: \cppinline{concat}, a monoid at the lengths that need no recursion, then the fold over the chain of a count under an arrow, then Fibonacci's matrix chain, which two declared laws fold in logarithmically many steps and the compiler partially evaluates.
Section~\ref{sec:limits} says where the arrows are partial, Section~\ref{sec:related} where the work sits, and the calculus is the appendix.

\section{From \Cpp{} to Jlt}
\label{sec:posture}

Jlt is standard \Cpp{23} under three rules, not a dialect and not a library; the compiler enforces some of the rules, and we say which.
Each rule has a listing of a few lines, verbatim from the repository's test up to formatting and the definitions a listing omits or the test imports; the LLVM IR is Clang~23's output at \texttt{-O2 -fno-unroll-loops}, with names shortened.

\paragraph{Rule 1, Substitution: values and pure functions.}
\phantomsection\label{def:rule1}
Every type a Jlt program passes around is \cppinline{std::semiregular}: default constructible, copyable, and nothing else is assumed.
Every function is \cppinline{constexpr} and depends on its arguments alone.\footnote{A \cppinline{constexpr} function may still change its own locals: constant evaluation permits modifying only objects whose lifetime began within it, so no mutation can leak, which is the encapsulated state of \cppinline{runST}~\cite{launchbury1994lazy}.
At runtime the linter of Section~\ref{sec:limits} holds the body to the same condition by closing each channel a mutation could leak through: parameters, results, the object, statics, globals, \cppinline{volatile}, boxes, and escaping closures that capture by reference.}
What the rule conserves is Leibniz's law~\cite{leibniz1686discours,leibniz1686discourse}, that equals cannot be told apart by any operation, at either binding time: a call may be replaced by its value, $e[f(x)] = e[v]$ whenever $f(x)$ evaluates to $v$.
In a programming language the law is referential transparency~\cite{strachey2000fundamental}, and a language where $a = b$ but $f(a) \neq f(b)$ for some $f$ has an equality one cannot reason with.

In Listing~\ref{lst:rule1}, \cppinline{const T} and \cppinline{const T&} are one parameter, the second an optimisation the translation erases, since nothing changes the value the reference denotes.
An arrow is a function made a value, and its laws are equations between values, so \cppinline{==} is where Leibniz's law meets the program: the kernel asks it of no type, the listings of every carrier a law is stated on.
The properties a law singles out, mono, epi and iso by cancellation, involution, the variances, are certificates declared or derived on the arrow, whose type names carrier and operation, so a certificate is about the signature~\cite{pierce1991basic}.
Two consequences keep that exact: a function returns a value, never a reference, and every member function, a call operator included, is \cppinline{const}.
The grammar of Appendix~\ref{app:fjlt} has neither a reference type nor a non-\cppinline{const} member, and the linter of Section~\ref{sec:limits} holds \Cpp{} to the same.
The compiler enforces the rule in part: a \cppinline{constexpr} function that reaches for state fails to evaluate at compile time, but nothing stops a caller from only ever calling it at runtime.

\paragraph{Rule 2, Abstraction: a type varies behaviour only through its concepts and traits.}
\phantomsection\label{def:rule2}
Generic code may do with a \cppinline{T} what \cppinline{T}'s concepts and traits say it can, and nothing else.
That is the premise of Wadler's free theorems~\cite{wadler1989theorems}, with concepts and traits in the place of type classes~\cite{wadler1989adhoc}, and what it conserves is the body's independence from the carrier: \cppinline{f<T>} is one generic term applied to \cppinline{T}'s dictionary, so it respects every relation the dictionary respects (Corollary~\ref{cor:parametricity}).

\begin{listing}[htbp]
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.47\linewidth}
\begin{cpp}
using namespace std;

template <typename T, typename Op>
constexpr T unit(Op) = delete;
template <typename T, typename Op>
constexpr bool associative(Op) = delete;

template <unsigned_integral T>
constexpr T unit(plus<T>) { return 0; }
template <unsigned_integral T>
constexpr bool associative(plus<T>) {
  return true;
}
template <typename T, typename Op>
  requires equality_comparable<T>
constexpr bool is_unit(const T& a, Op op) {
  return a == unit<T>(op);
}

template <typename T, typename Op>
concept IsMonoid =
    semiregular<T> &&
    requires(const T a, const T b) {
      { Op{}(a, b) } -> same_as<T>;
      { unit<T>(Op{}) } -> same_as<T>;
      { is_unit(a, Op{}) } -> same_as<bool>;
    } && associative<T>(Op{});
\end{cpp}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.5\linewidth}
\begin{cpp}
template <typename T, typename Op = plus<T>>
  requires IsMonoid<T, Op>
constexpr T concat() { return unit<T>(Op{}); }
template <typename T, typename Op = plus<T>>
  requires IsMonoid<T, Op>
constexpr T concat(T w) { return w; }
template <typename T, typename Op = plus<T>>
  requires IsMonoid<T, Op>
constexpr T concat(T v, T w) { return Op{}(v, w); }

static_assert(
concat<size_t>() == 0 &&
concat<size_t>(42uz) == 42 &&
concat<size_t>(40uz, 2uz) == 42 &&
concat<size_t, multiplies<size_t>>(6uz, 7uz)
    == 42 &&
concat<string>() == "" &&
concat<string>("ab"s, "c"s) == "abc" &&
concat<bool, logical_or<bool>>(false, true) &&
concat<bool, logical_and<bool>>(true, true) &&
concat<Sections<size_t>, compose>()(42uz)
    == 42 &&
concat<Sections<size_t>, compose>(

\end{cpp}

  & & f(n-1) \arrow[d]
  & \\
  \llap{$M$:\quad}
  & \bullet
  & & \circ \arrow[ll, "h"']
  & & \cdots \arrow[ll, "h"']
  & & \circ \arrow[ll, "h"']
  & & e \arrow[ll, "h"']
\end{tikzcd}
\caption{Where each structure lives.
Seed: after Lambek~\cite{lambek1968fixpoint}, the structure map $[0, \mathit{succ}] : \mathbf{1} + X \to X$ of the naturals is an isomorphism.
Its inverse $\mathit{prior} : X \to \mathbf{1} + X$ steps down and always ends.
Peano's $\mathit{succ}$~\cite{peano1889arithmetices}, dashed, never does.
The user supplies the point $n : \mathbf{1} \to X$ and $\mathit{prior}$.
The chain is what $\mathit{prior}$ reaches from $n$: the $n$ elements below it, not $n$ itself.
So $n$ names the chain's cardinality, and $0$ the empty chain.
Order: after Dedekind~\cite{dedekind1888zahlen}, a chain is a set closed under a map into itself.
The chain of an element is its forward orbit, here the chain of $\mathit{prior}\,n$.
The carrier needs neither an order nor an origin.
Reachability under $\mathit{prior}$ orders the chain, and its bottom is where $\mathit{prior}$ has nothing.
Algebra: $f$ lands in $S$, and the fold in $M$.
In the simplest case $S = M$ is a monoid.
A monoid $S$ may also act on $M$, and a semiring on a semimodule, which adds distributivity.
Fold: a list has two folds~\cite{meijer1991functional,gibbons1996third}.
The one along the walk, $h : M \times S \to M$, runs from $e$ at the seed's end through the intermediate values, $\circ$, to the result, $\bullet$.
It needs no list: it is a stream and its iterator~\cite{stepanov2009elements}.
With $\mathit{succ}$ as well, $\mathit{succ} >> \mathit{prior} = \eta$, the chain is a zipper~\cite{huet1997zipper}.
With offsets it is an array~\cite[\S 2.2.2]{knuth1997art1}.
These are the standard's forward, bidirectional and random-access iterators~\cite{cpp23standard}.
}
\label{fig:structures}
\end{figure}

\cppinline{concat} uses only what its constraint names, so it is a function of the monoid's record.
A \Cpp{} compiler checks a template's body at instantiation, against the type supplied, and not at definition, against the constraint, which is why this is a rule here and not a theorem.

\paragraph{Rule 3, Descent: recursion is structural.}
\phantomsection\label{def:rule3}
A Jlt program recurses on a structure whose residue, what remains to be folded, gets smaller at every step and so reaches its end.
The step is Figure~\ref{fig:structures}'s $\mathit{prior}$, which steps down and has nothing at the bottom, or another step down the same order, as halving is (Listing~\ref{lst:laws}), on an order with no infinite descent.
What the rule conserves is that inductive argument: structural induction~\cite{burstall1969proving}, a language all of whose programs end by it being Turner's total functional programming~\cite{turner2004total}, and the fold's universal property its equation~\cite{meijer1991functional}, $h = \mathit{fold}\,a$ exactly when $h \circ \mathit{in} = a \circ F\,h$.\footnote{$F$ is one layer of the data type with a hole for the rest, for a list of words $F\,X = 1 + \mathit{Word} \times X$; $\mathit{in}$ builds one more layer, the constructors $\mathit{nil}$ and $\mathit{cons}$; $a$, the algebra, says what to do with one layer once the rest is folded; and $F\,h$ applies $h$ to the rest only.
For \cppinline{concat} the equation is its definition, $h(\mathit{nil}) = \mathit{unit}$ and $h(\mathit{cons}(w, r)) = w \oplus h(r)$, and \emph{exactly when} says that \cppinline{concat} is the only function that satisfies both.}
Meijer et al.\ take their recursion operators in CPO, where one list type holds the finite, the infinite and the partial lists, and a fold over it need not end; Jlt takes them for data only, the initial algebras in Set, where a list in a type is finite as an initial algebra and the chain in a value is finite by the descent, and codata is out of scope: a coalgebra is admitted as a generator bounded by a count, never as a value.
The structure lives in a \emph{type} (a typed term, an array of known length), or in a \emph{value}.
In both cases, an iteration that can be mapped onto the chain of Figure~\ref{fig:structures} contains no loop and terminates, whether the chain is held as one count or as the elements of an array.
In the terms of Meijer et al., \cppinline{concat} over a list is a catamorphism.
The chain is an anamorphism, $\mathit{prior}$ unfolded from the seed and delimited where it has nothing.
\cppinline{concat} over the chain is a hylomorphism, and the chain is never built as a list.

\section{From Jlt to Featherweight Jlt}
\label{sec:translation:adequacy}

This section is the $-$sugar arrow of Figure~\ref{fig:arrows}, between the rules of Section~\ref{sec:posture} and the kernel's grammar of Section~\ref{sec:translation}: which construction of Jlt becomes which term of the kernel, each lemma left to Appendix~\ref{app:fjlt:closure}.
Desugaring rewrites a program into one that uses only the kernel's constructions and computes the same value, as the \Cpp{} standard defines a lambda as a class with a call operator; no tool runs it.
Tables~\ref{tab:closure} and~\ref{tab:closure:decl} state it construction by construction: a condition, and the kernel term it yields.

A branch on a type, the sugar that matters most, becomes a family of generic functions ordered by specificity (Appendix~\ref{app:fjlt:typing}), which Theorem~\ref{thm:coherence} keeps coherent.
Listing~\ref{lst:laws}'s two specialisations, each licensed by a law, compose under it.
The other sugar is routine (Table~\ref{tab:closure}).

The theorems of Section~\ref{sec:guarantees} are about FJlt, and they reach a \Cpp{} program on two conditions, which hold in different ways.

\emph{Every FJlt program is a Jlt program, with the same meaning.}
This direction holds by construction (Appendix~\ref{app:fjlt:syntax}): the grammar of Figure~\ref{fig:fjlt-syntax} is \Cpp{} syntax, FJlt's evaluation is constant evaluation restricted to it, each construct keeps the standard's rule, and the standard library pieces the grammar admits keep their standard meaning.
Constant evaluation refuses undefined behaviour, so at compile time an FJlt program cannot go wrong silently; at run time it can (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\emph{A Jlt program written in the grammar of Figure~\ref{fig:fjlt-syntax} is an FJlt program.}
This direction is not a theorem, because the compiler does not check two of the rules.
It is also conditional on the grammar, because \Cpp{} has constructions FJlt lacks, and a Jlt program may use them.

\subsection{Instances with contexts}
\label{sec:translation:contexts}

An instance may itself require instances: the unit of a pair is the pair of units, provided both components have one.
Listing~\ref{lst:rule2}'s instance for the unsigned integrals is the simplest case, one context on one carrier.
In \Cpp{} that is a generic function with a \cppinline{requires} clause, and the clause is the context.
Under translation it is a function from the components' records to the composite's:
\begin{minted}{text}
pair_unit  =  Λ A. Λ B. λ da : MonoidDict A OpA. λ db : MonoidDict B OpB.
                (da.unit, db.unit)
\end{minted}
The compiler applies such functions outside in, as often as the composite nests.
The recursion ends, because a context can only mention proper parts of the type it is for.\footnote{The termination conditions on instance contexts are studied by Sulzmann et al.~\cite{sulzmann2007understanding}.
GHC calls them the Paterson conditions.
The FJlt syntax enforces them by construction, since a context can only mention the type arguments of the operation being specialised.}

\section{From System~F Back to Featherweight Jlt}
\label{sec:guarantees}
% The three theorems about the translation of Section~\ref{sec:translation}
% and Appendix~\ref{app:fjlt}, stated after the exhibits so that each can
% point at one.  Proof sketches in Appendix~\ref{app:fjlt:theorems}; the
% adequacy argument (C++ -> FJlt) is Section~\ref{sec:translation:adequacy}
% and is not a theorem.

The theorems rest on FJlt alone.
Their proofs (Appendix~\ref{app:fjlt:theorems}) are inductions over the grammar, the typing and the translation of FJlt, and never mention the three rules.
The rules are not the hypotheses of the theorems.
They are what makes a \Cpp{} program a term of the calculus the theorems are about, and that step is the argument of Section~\ref{sec:translation:adequacy}.

\begin{theorem}[Type preservation]
\label{thm:preservation}
If $\Gamma \vdash e : \tau$ in FJlt, then $\llbracket \Gamma \rrbracket \vdash \llbracket e \rrbracket : \llbracket \tau \rrbracket$ in System~F.
\end{theorem}

Each \cppinline{requires} clause has become a record parameter.
At each call, the instance the compiler resolved supplies a record of the type that parameter demands.

\begin{theorem}[Simulation]
\label{thm:simulation}
If $e \longrightarrow e'$ in FJlt, then $\llbracket e \rrbracket \longrightarrow^{+} \llbracket e' \rrbracket$ in System~F, and values translate to values.
For closed terms of base type, $e$ evaluates to $v$ if and only if $\llbracket e \rrbracket$ reduces to $\llbracket v \rrbracket$.
\end{theorem}

What a \cppinline{static_assert} sees is what the translation computes, and, by Rule~1, what the runtime computes.
Section~\ref{sec:binding} is this theorem run twice.
The theorem also fixes the notion of equality, which equational reasoning presupposes and which is a question of its own~\cite{mazur2008equal}: two closed terms of a carrier are equal when they evaluate to the same value, by behaviour and not by identity, and Leibniz's law of Rule~1 holds by substitution.
That is why Rule~1 asks \cppinline{std::semiregular} and not \cppinline{std::regular}: no \cppinline{==} is asked of a value, a carrier's \cppinline{==} is one more operation of its signature where a shape asks for it, and a function object, a value under Rule~1, has none.

\begin{theorem}[Coherence]
\label{thm:coherence}
The translation is a function of the program: two resolutions of the same constraint at the same concrete types yield the same record.
\end{theorem}

In \Cpp{} this is overload resolution together with the one-definition rule: at concrete types a specialisation is unique, or the program is ill-formed.
It gives ``the unit of \cppinline{+} at \cppinline{size_t}'' one meaning in a program, so a law can be stated about it, and it lets laws compose: two overloads written apart are chosen the same way wherever they are called, as the two behind Listing~\ref{lst:laws} are.

\begin{corollary}[Termination]
\label{cor:termination}
Every FJlt program terminates.
\end{corollary}

It follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:simulation} and strong normalisation of the target~\cite{girard1989proofs}.
Every step of the program is at least one step of its image, and the image cannot step forever.
The count in Rule~3 is therefore not a guard against non-termination.
It is how a bound is said.

\begin{corollary}[Parametricity]
\label{cor:parametricity}
A generic FJlt function satisfies the free theorem of its System~F type.
\end{corollary}

By Theorem~\ref{thm:preservation} and Reynolds' abstraction theorem~\cite{reynolds1983types}.
\cppinline{concat} at a monoid can only combine the words by \texttt{op}, starting from \texttt{unit}, which is the free theorem of a fold.
It is also why an instance with a context (Section~\ref{sec:translation:contexts}) can read a composite's law off its components' records.
The composite cannot do anything the components' records do not provide.

    const bool set = ((n >> (k - 1)) & 1U) != 0;
    return prefix(set ? op(twice, x) : twice,

}

\begin{corollary}[Type soundness]
\label{cor:soundness}
A well-typed closed FJlt term of base type evaluates to a value.
\end{corollary}

This is the theorem a reader of Featherweight Java~\cite{igarashi2001featherweight} expects first, and here it is cheap once the rest is in place.
A closed well-typed term is a value or takes a step (progress, Lemma~\ref{lem:progress}), and it cannot step forever (Corollary~\ref{cor:termination}), so it reaches a value.
What the translation adds is the second half of Theorem~\ref{thm:simulation}: the value reached is the value the System~F image computes.

\paragraph{Certified and inferred laws.}
A program declares few laws and infers the rest.
A declared law is an axiom, its certificate evaluated where a record is built.
A law at a construction is derived from the laws of its parts, through an instance with a context, by the entailment of qualified types~\cite{jones1994qualified}: that a finite power of a semiring is a semimodule over it is inferred, not declared (Appendix~\ref{app:fjlt:inferred}).
The inference is local in Pierce and Turner's sense~\cite{pierce2000local}: a law flows from a term's immediate parts to the term and never back from its use, a synthesized attribute~\cite{knuth1968semantics}.
They infer type arguments and Jlt infers laws, so the likeness is in the flow of information, not in what is inferred.
By Theorem~\ref{thm:coherence} each inferred law is unique, and the conditions on contexts that end instance search~\cite{sulzmann2007understanding} end the inference.
As type checking rules out classes of errors without proving a program correct, law inference licenses classes of optimisations without deciding program equivalence, which Rice's theorem places out of reach~\cite{rice1953classes}.
Each optimisation is local, a rewrite at the node whose law licenses it, and so mechanical; the global one, the best equivalent program, is the undecidable one.
Since the laws are the application's, not the machine's, so are the optimisations they license.

\section{Partial Evaluation at Build Time}
\label{sec:binding}

Every function in this paper has run twice already.
The test behind the listings calls the same functions at runtime, on values it did not have at compile time.
Rule~1 is what makes that unremarkable: a \cppinline{constexpr} function of its arguments alone has no binding time of its own.\footnote{A \cppinline{consteval} function is evaluated only at compile time, so it has one binding time, which is why Rule~1 asks \cppinline{constexpr} and not \cppinline{consteval}.}
Theorem~\ref{thm:simulation} says the two evaluations agree, which is the presumption of this section: moving a call from compile time to run time changes where it is evaluated, not what it evaluates to.
Both are partial evaluation~\cite{jones1993partial} at build time: the compiler evaluates what it can and leaves the processor the rest.
Listing~\ref{lst:desugar} follows one program down both legs.
With $n$ free, each leg evaluates what it can and keeps the fold over $n$, which only the run time can finish.
The System~F term gets there by two equations outside the appendix's reductions, fusion and dropping an unread position.
What remains is the loop the optimiser emits, read as a function after Appel~\cite{appel1998ssa}.
With $n$ given, the term evaluates to $55$, and so does the code: Figure~\ref{fig:arrows}'s bottom edge on one program.

\paragraph{The two evaluators are different programs.}
In constant evaluation no optimiser runs: the front end interprets a fold one step at a time, and the optimiser that reduces Listing~\ref{lst:desugar}'s matrices to two additions sees only the code emitted for run time.
So at compile time only a declared law shortens a fold, chosen by resolution, as for Listing~\ref{lst:laws}'s overloads: it buys at build time what the optimiser finds at run time by itself.

Not every algorithm converges, so Jlt allows a \emph{budget}, a counter beside the structure's own count, as the compiler has one; exhausting either is never a value (Appendix~\ref{app:fjlt:ssa}).\footnote{The compiler's budgets apply at compile time only, Jlt's at both binding times, and they are the compiler's to set: raised, the refused fold of $10^8$ copies compiles and holds.}

\paragraph{The same laws license parallelism.}
A parallel \cppinline{concat} is one more overload, licensed by \cppinline{IsCommutativeMonoid}.
The branch on the binding time that selects it desugars to its compile-time branch, because the same law proves the branches agree (Appendix~\ref{app:fjlt:consteval}).

\Cpp{} passes no dictionaries but instantiates, so the translation models what a constrained call means, not what the compiler emits.
Instantiation is partial evaluation of the dictionary-passing term at a static type, so the two agree on every closed term, and parametricity holds of every instantiation (Appendix~\ref{app:fjlt:ssa}).

\section{Known Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

Table~\ref{tab:posture} says which instrument holds each rule at build time.

\subsection{Limits of principle}

\paragraph{The compiler does not check a template's body against its constraints.}
Definition checking was the half of the \Cpp{0x} design~\cite{gregor2006concepts} that \Cpp{20} did not take up (Section~\ref{sec:intro}).
So Rules~2 and~3 are posture: they are held outside the compiler, and no tool turns them into the compiler's verdict.

\paragraph{Lookup stops at \cppinline{std}.}
A name in a template is looked up twice: at the template's definition, among the declarations visible there, and at instantiation only by argument-dependent lookup, in the namespaces of the argument types, which for a standard type is \cppinline{std}, where no instance of ours lives.
So an instance declared after the shape is found only as a specialisation of a primary template the shape names, as Listing~\ref{lst:rule2}'s deleted \cppinline{unit} is, and a constrained overload must precede the shape.
This is a constraint on declaration order that the translation's instance resolution does not have, and FJlt resolves it by fiat: an instance is a specialisation of the trait, found by the trait's name.


\paragraph{A shape knows the laws someone declared.}
\Cpp{}'s own words are that a type \emph{satisfies} a concept, which the compiler decides, and \emph{models} it, which it does not~\cite[{\texttt{res.on.requirements}}]{cpp23standard}, and the Core Guidelines ask that a concept carry meaningful semantics for that reason~\cite[T.20]{cppcoreguidelines}.\footnote{The standard calls the first kind of requirement syntactic and the second semantic.
That split is between what the compiler decides and what it leaves to the programmer, and it does not coincide with the split of programming-language theory between static and dynamic semantics~\cite{pierce2002tapl}, whose static semantics, type checking, falls on the syntactic side here.}
A law is what modelling asks beyond satisfying, and the posture makes it a declared certificate, a specialisation the compiler finds but does not check against the operation.
So \cppinline{false} means \emph{not known}, not \emph{no}, and the same declaration is the only refusal available for a carrier that passes a shape by signature and breaks its law by value.\footnote{\cppinline{*} on \cppinline{size_t} is commutative, but nobody in the listings certifies it, so \cppinline{IsCommutativeMonoid<size_t, multiplies<size_t>>} is refused.
\cppinline{double} under \cppinline{+} has the signature of a monoid, but \cppinline{+} is not associative and the unit law fails at NaN, and nobody certifies it.}

\paragraph{Undefined behaviour has one binding time.}
Constant evaluation refuses it and run time does not (Table~\ref{tab:binding}), the one way the two evaluations of Theorem~\ref{thm:simulation} can disagree in \Cpp{} where they cannot in FJlt, and the reason the listings' carriers are unsigned.
A Jlt program on \cppinline{int} is a Jlt program only where its arithmetic cannot overflow, a fact no rule checks.\footnote{A signed integer is a chain under \cppinline{<} and a bounded lattice under \cppinline{min} and \cppinline{max}, but no monoid under \cppinline{+} or \cppinline{*}, since overflow is undefined.}

\paragraph{The compiler has its own limits.}
Table~\ref{tab:binding} lists two, steps and call depth, and the template instantiation depth bounds both binding times.
Rule~3 keeps them predictable, because every fold's length is in the program: a count, which meets no depth limit, or the depth of a term, one instantiation or one call per nesting.

\subsection{Limits of this presentation}

\paragraph{The linter is sound and incomplete.}
The repository's linter, run in continuous integration on the compiler's own front end and documented there, checks Rule~3 for every instantiated body, Rule~1's syntactic part, and Rule~2 at the level of names: a body may use, on a value of parameter type, only what a declared constraint names.
A finding is a breach or a gap in its reading of the standard's concepts, and its silence is not a proof.
Review complements it: an agentic reviewer reads every pull request against the three rules, and the listings and the library's core are held at zero on all three to the extent the two instruments reach.

\paragraph{The adequacy argument covers part of the library.}
Tables~\ref{tab:closure} and~\ref{tab:closure:decl} are measured complete for the \texttt{category} module and the listings only.
Other modules are held by the same linter at a count that only goes down.


\paragraph{The standard library is trusted.}
The listings compile with Clang~23 against two standard libraries, trusted as every \Cpp{} program trusts them; the youngest piece they use, the folds over ranges, is complete since libc++~23~\cite{libcxx2026status}.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Type classes.}
Dictionary passing is Wadler and Blott's~\cite{wadler1989adhoc}.
Table~\ref{tab:dictionary} is that translation with \Cpp{}'s names in the left column, and the row without a counterpart there, structural satisfaction, is the difference Bernardy et al.~\cite{bernardy2010generic} place at the centre of their comparison of concepts and type classes.
Coherence, that a program has one meaning however its constraints are resolved, is where the designs diverge: Racordon et al.~\cite{racordon2025coherence} survey how Haskell, Swift, Rust and Scala keep or trade it, between unique instances and context-dependent resolution.
FJlt takes the strict side by construction, since an instance is a function specialisation and the one-definition rule admits one per head.
Theorem~\ref{thm:coherence} is that choice stated as a property of the translation, and the price, that instances for families of types declared after their shape do not fit, is in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.
Instance resolution is the inference half of type classes, Hindley--Milner's~\cite{milner1978theory,damas1982principal} extended with predicates~\cite{jones1994qualified}; FJlt infers no types, since \Cpp{} deduces them, but it infers laws the same way.
Rust and Swift also check a generic body against its bounds: a comparison on an unconstrained type parameter is a compile error in both, with the missing bound named in the message~\cite{klabnik2023rust,swift2026generics}.
That is Rule~2 as a compiler check, the half \Cpp{20} dropped, and the posture, together with the linter of Section~\ref{sec:limits}, is what recovering it by hand costs.

\paragraph{Concepts, and the calculus next door.}
The nearest neighbour of Appendix~\ref{app:fjlt} is Siek and Lumsdaine's $F^G$~\cite{siek2005essential,siek2004formalization}: System~F with concepts and models, translated into System~F by dictionary passing, with the translation proved sound in Isabelle.
It was written for the concepts then proposed for \Cpp{0x}~\cite{gregor2006concepts,dosreis2006specifying}, which checked template bodies and were removed~\cite{stroustrup2009remove}, leaving the gap Rule~2 names.
$F^G$'s models are nominal and can be declared for whole families of types, which is what the library behind this paper's listings needs after its shapes, where FJlt's function-specialisation instances are not found (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).
FJlt is the smaller calculus, and the price of being a subset of a shipped language.
Of the generic-programming features Garcia et al.~\cite{garcia2007extended} compare across eight languages, the two \Cpp{} then lacked, multi-type concepts and retroactive modelling, are what Listing~\ref{lst:rule2} exercises.

\paragraph{Featherweight calculi.}
Featherweight Java~\cite{igarashi2001featherweight} set the pattern: a core calculus small enough to prove things about, with a syntax that is a subset of the host language's.
Featherweight Go~\cite{griesemer2020featherweight} follows the pattern for structural subtyping and monomorphisation, and \emph{Generic Go to Go}~\cite{ellis2022generic} proves a dictionary-passing translation correct by a bisimulation.
That is the genre Section~\ref{sec:guarantees} is in, preservation and simulation of a translation, with soundness, termination and parametricity inherited from the target rather than proved from scratch.

\paragraph{Formal semantics of \Cpp{}.}
CoreC++~\cite{wasserrab2006operational} gives an operational semantics and a machine-checked type safety proof for multiple inheritance; Ramananandro, Dos Reis and Leroy~\cite{ramananandro2011layout,ramananandro2012construction} mechanise object layout, construction and destruction in Coq.
These formalise the part of \Cpp{} that Rule~1 excludes, objects with identity and lifetime, and none covers templates, concepts or constant evaluation, so FJlt is a fragment with its own grammar rather than a subset of a formalised \Cpp{}.

\paragraph{What System~F supplies.}
Strong normalisation is Girard's \cite{girard1989proofs}, parametricity Reynolds' \cite{reynolds1983types}, and data types as their own folds B\"ohm and Berarducci's \cite{bohm1985automatic}: the appendix uses the three as black boxes, which is the point of translating into System~F.

\paragraph{Functional programming in \Cpp{}, and the other direction.}
Functional programming in \Cpp{} is practised and taught: in a book~\cite{cukic2018functional}, in a metaprogramming library~\cite{chagnon2016hana}, and in category theory written in \Cpp{}~\cite{milewski2019category}; none states a fragment or proves anything of it.
The opposite route to the same end verifies a program in a proof assistant and extracts C from it, as Low* does from F*~\cite{protzenko2017verified}; Jlt keeps the programmer in \Cpp{} and puts the proof in the translation.

\paragraph{Coding standards and profiles.}
The documents an audit of \Cpp{} works from are rule catalogues: MISRA \Cpp{}~\cite{misra2023cpp} and the SEI CERT standard~\cite{ballman2016cert} list hundreds of rules, each justified by the defects it prevents, with no statement of what a compliant program computes.
Jlt is narrower, a pure computational core with no I/O, and so complements them rather than replacing them; where they meet they can differ, as MISRA forbids recursion except in constant evaluation and Rule~3 admits structural recursion on the strength of a termination argument.
The nearest relatives are the safety profiles proposed for \Cpp{26}~\cite{sutter2025profiles}, named subsets the compiler would enforce, and the criticism that a profile should come with the invariant it upholds~\cite{muller2025principled}: Jlt reads as a functional profile, and its three rules state theirs.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Three rules on standard \Cpp{23} were stated, and a program that keeps them is, up to the gaps Section~\ref{sec:translation:adequacy} names, a term of a core calculus whose syntax is that \Cpp{}.
The calculus translates into System~F by dictionary passing, and the translation preserves types and evaluation and is a function of the program (Theorems~\ref{thm:preservation} to~\ref{thm:coherence}), so a program of the calculus terminates, is parametric and is sound (Corollaries~\ref{cor:termination} to~\ref{cor:soundness}).

Two of the three rules are held by the author, a linter and an agentic reviewer, not by the compiler (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

Four things follow as work not done here.
Completing the linter into a checker, with definition checking over types, arities and the full constraint language, as the \Cpp{0x} design had it.
Certifying that a run-time shortcut agrees with the fold it replaces, which a branch on the binding time leaves to a law or a reader: the third homomorphism theorem characterises when a fold can be split~\cite{gibbons1996third}, and a shape could declare it.
A mechanisation of the proofs, the aspirational end, as Siek and Lumsdaine did for $F^G$ in Isabelle~\cite{siek2004formalization}.
And laws that are computed with rather than only declared, which needs terms and their composition.

Declared laws, and the laws inferred from them, license optimisations of the application's own algebra, as types rule out errors: by a sound check, not a decision of equivalence.
The same laws are those a query optimiser applies to relational algebra~\cite{codd1970relational}, so the posture reads, the other way round, as an intensional SQL: queries over relations given by their laws, not by their rows.
The laws survive the trip into Python, up to the compile-time evaluation: a session that composes and counts through the bindings runs the overloads the laws license, for those who would rather tinker than compile.
Nothing was added to the language, the compiler or the library: what was added is a boundary, inside which a mainstream compiler checks claims of a modest but real kind about programs that then run unchanged, for the people who already write \Cpp{23}.
